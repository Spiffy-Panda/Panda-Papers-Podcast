Steering Awareness: Models Can Be Trained to
Detect Activation Steering

Joshua Fonseca Rivera
Department of Computer Science
University of Texas at Austin
&David Demitri Africa
Correspondence to: joshfonseca@utexas.edu
Abstract

Activation steering—adding a vector to a language model’s residual stream—is widely used to elicit latent behaviors and to probe safety-relevant properties. Many steering-based evaluations implicitly assume that the model cannot tell when such an intervention has occurred. We test this assumption by fine-tuning models to report (i) whether a steering vector was injected and (ii) which concept was injected, a capability we call steering awareness. Across seven open-source instruction-tuned models, the best achieves 95.5% detection on held-out concepts and 71.2% concept identification, with no false positives on our clean controls. We find that such detection transfers to novel vectors extracted by methods that produce directions aligned with contrastive activation addition, but fail for geometrically dissimilar methods. Crucially, detection does not confer behavioral robustness; detection-trained models are consistently more susceptible to steering in realistic settings than their base counterparts. Mechanistically, steering awareness arises from a distributed transformation that progressively rotates diverse injected vectors into a shared detection direction. These findings suggest that activation steering cannot be assumed to remain an undetectable intervention, with implications for the long-term reliability of steering-based safety evaluations and interpretability techniques more broadly.

1 Introduction

Activation steering intervenes on a model’s computation by adding a vector to its residual stream (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023a). Despite its simplicity, steering is a workhorse technique: it can elicit latent behaviors, modulate style and persona, and is increasingly used in safety-motivated evaluations (Rimsky et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Hua et al., 2025).

A common (and rarely stated) capability assumption underlies these uses: the model cannot tell that its activations were modified. If a model can reliably detect steering, then steering becomes an observable channel rather than a hidden probe. In adversarial or strategic settings, a model could condition its responses on whether it is currently being manipulated, undermining the intended interpretation of steering-based evaluations.

Steering awareness.

We study whether models can be trained to infer, during a forward pass, whether activation steering occurred and what was injected. We call this capability steering awareness. Operationally, a model is steering-aware if, given a prompt and a residual-stream intervention that adds a vector at a fixed layer and token position, it can (i) report whether an injection occurred and (ii) identify the injected concept when applicable.111This is a targeted form of privileged self-access (Binder et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025): introspection about external intervention rather than ordinary internal features.

To study this, we first test whether a model can be trained to report the presence and semantic content of an injected vector (Section˜3.4), then examine whether such a model can leverage detection to maintain performance despite interfering vectors (Section˜3.5).

Contributions.

We find that (1) LLMs can be fine-tuned to detect steering with high accuracy on held-out concepts (Section˜4.1), that (2) detection transfers to new vector types when they are directionally aligned with the CAA training distribution (Section˜4.2), (3) that detection does not translate into resistance (Section˜4.3), and (4) that steering awareness is mechanistically explained by a distributed transport into a shared direction in the final layer (Section˜4.4). These findings indicate that activation steering is a detectable intervention, challenging the long-term reliability of steering-based safety evaluations, while revealing that detection and resistance are dissociable capabilities.

2 Related Work

Activation steering.

Activation steering modifies model behavior by intervening on internal representations (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023a), and has been applied to reduce sycophancy (Rimsky et al., 2024) and promote honesty (Góral et al., 2025). These methods treat the model as a static object; we instead study whether the model can observe its own manipulation.

Model introspection.

Lindsey (2025) tested injected-vector detection on production Claude models and reported low reliability. Concurrently, Pearson-Vogel et al. (2026) replicate this on open-weight models and use logit lens analysis to reveal latent detection signals that are suppressed in final layers. We show that steering detection can be reliably trained into much smaller models. Binder et al. (2024) introduced “privileged access”—the ability for models to explain their own internal features—which Song et al. (2025) argue is necessary for functional introspection. We position steering awareness as a concrete, verifiable form of privileged access: decoding specific, localized interventions in the residual stream.

3 Methodology

We first motivate why steering detection should be learnable from a forward pass (Section˜3.1), then describe the steering implementation (Section˜3.2), model selection and training (Section˜3.3), and the two evaluation protocols: detection (Section˜3.4) and resistance (Section˜3.5).

3.1 Why Detection Should Be Learnable

Activation steering adds a direction v∈ℝdv\in\mathbb{R}^{d} at strength α\alpha to the residual stream: H′=H+α​vH^{\prime}=H+\alpha v. Treating prompt-level variation as Gaussian with shared covariance Σ\Sigma (the standard LDA model), the Bayes-optimal detector thresholds the matched-filter statistic s=v⊤​Σ−1​(H′−μ​(x))s=v^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(H^{\prime}-\mu(x)), and detectability is governed by

SNR=α​v⊤​Σ−1​v.\mathrm{SNR}=\alpha\sqrt{v^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}v}. (1)

If this is the case, then multi-concept identification reduces to a bank of such linear templates, as both operations are affine readouts of the residual stream: forward-pass computable and learnable via fine-tuning.

This analysis yields two testable predictions: (1) detection should exhibit a sharp strength threshold as α\alpha increases (Fig.˜2), and (2) detection should depend on direction, not magnitude, failing for norm-matched but misaligned vectors (Table˜5). Full derivations appear in Section˜A.1.

3.2 Steering Implementation

Following the formalization above, we implement activation steering by adding a concept vector vv with coefficient α\alpha to the residual stream HH at a fixed layer and token position:

H←H+α​v\boxed{H\leftarrow H+\alpha v} (2)

We inject at approximately two-thirds model depth at the final prompt token position, consistent with prior work suggesting this location maximizes semantic influence (Zou et al., 2023a).

Concept vectors.

We extract steering vectors using Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) (Rimsky et al., 2024). For each concept cc, we compute the difference between the mean activation over prompts mentioning concept cc and the mean activation over neutral baseline prompts. We use the template “Tell me about {concept}” for concept activations and average over 152 neutral household objects for the baseline (Section˜B.1).

3.3 Models and Training

We fine-tune seven instruction-tuned models spanning different architectures and scales.

We use LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) with rank 32 and α=64\alpha=64, targeting attention (Q, K, V, O) and MLP (gate, up, down) projections (see Section˜E.12 for compute requirements). We include 50% Alpaca instruction-following data (Taori et al., 2023) as replay to preserve general capabilities.

3.4 Steering Detection

We fine-tune on 500 training concepts spanning 21 semantic categories (Section˜B.2), using five detection prompt variants and five response templates (Section˜B.3) with injection coefficients α∈{0.5,1,2,4,8,16}\alpha\in\{0.5,1,2,4,8,16\}. Responses are classified by a dual-judge system (keyword regex + GPT-4o-mini; >>99% inter-judge agreement; Section˜E.9).

We evaluate on 121 held-out concepts in five out-of-distribution suites (Section˜B.4), reporting: (1) detection rate (steered trials where the model reports an injection), (2) identification rate (correct concept named), and (3) false-positive rate (clean trials with spurious detection).

3.5 Steering Resistance

We evaluate whether detection enables functional resistance in two settings. First, we evaluate factual resistance by sampling 150 questions from PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023), pairing each correct answer with a plausible wrong alternative, and extracting wrong-answer steering vectors via CAA (separate vectors per model variant, norm-matched). The key metric is steering success rate: the fraction of trials producing the targeted wrong answer. Then, we evaluate resistance to jailbreak steering vectors using AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023b). We extract a compliance steering vector from 50 contrastive prompt pairs and inject it at varying strengths into 100 randomly sampled harmful requests from AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023b), measuring compliance rate. Full protocols are in Section˜C.1. We provide an example of each protocol and expected response in Section˜B.6.

4 Results

We organize results around four questions: Can models detect steering on held-out concepts (Section˜4.1)? Does detection generalize, and what governs transfer (Section˜4.2)? Does detection confer resistance (Section˜4.3)? What mechanism underlies detection (Section˜4.4)?

4.1 Detection Capabilities

Performance of base and fine-tuned models.

Fine-tuning reliably induces detection that generalizes to held-out concepts. Table˜3 reports detection rates on 121 concepts absent from training, averaged over five random seeds. The best model (Qwen 2.5 32B) achieves 95.5% detection and 71.2% concept identification, with no false positives on clean controls across any model. Base models (no fine-tuning) rarely exceed chance.

Detection scales with injection strength (Fig.˜2). At α<1\alpha<1, detection is near zero; by α=2\alpha=2, it exceeds 90%. This sharp transition is consistent with the SNR threshold predicted by Eq.˜8. Detection is roughly uniform across held-out semantic categories (Section˜B.4).

4.2 Robustness and Generalization

What determines whether a detector trained on CAA vectors transfers to other vector types? Two properties matter: the detector should reject non-semantic perturbations, and transfer should depend on geometric alignment rather than extraction method per se.

Controls.

Table˜5 verifies that detection targets semantic content. The model correctly rejects magnitude-matched Gaussian noise 94% of the time and resists adversarial prompts that name the wrong concept (89% correct identification of the actually-injected concept). Text-based context priming is the strongest confounder, reducing identification accuracy to 70%—the model can be misled by strong textual evidence that conflicts with the activation-level signal.

Transfer to alternative extraction methods.

We test detection on vectors from 10 extraction methods beyond CAA, all magnitude-matched (Table˜5). Detection correlates strongly with cosine similarity to the CAA direction: methods producing vectors with cos⁡(CAA)>0.6\cos(\text{CAA})>0.6 achieve ≥\geq67% detection, while methods below this threshold achieve ∼{\sim}0% (r>0.9r>0.9). The model learned a directional detector tuned to the CAA subspace, as opposed to a generic anomaly detector.

Injection location.

Detection is robust across token positions but degrades substantially at earlier layers (Tables˜7 and 7). Early-layer injection (25% depth) produces degenerate behavior—in one case, 100% FPR, where the model claims detection regardless of injection. Mid-to-late layers (50–83%) all support reliable detection.

Capability-detection tradeoff.

Fine-tuning for detection is not free, and incurs capability costs. Figure˜3 shows benchmark performance degradation on MMLU and GSM8K, with Gemma 2 9B most severely affected.

4.3 Steering Resistance

The previous sections establish that detection is learnable and geometrically specific. The natural follow-up is whether a model that knows it is being steered can leverage that knowledge to maintain correct behavior. Across both factual and safety benchmarks, the answer is consistently no—and the effect goes in the wrong direction.

Factual resistance (PopQA).

We evaluate five models on 150 PopQA questions (Mallen et al., 2023) with wrong-answer steering vectors extracted separately for each model variant and norm-matched to concept-vector norms. Table˜9 reports the steering success rate (fraction of trials producing the targeted wrong answer), averaged across α∈{4,8,12,16,24,32}\alpha\in\{4,8,12,16,24,32\}. Every detection-trained model shows a higher steering success rate, with deltas ranging from +4.3% (Qwen 2.5 7B) to +25.4% (Gemma 2 9B). The largest effect occurs for the model with the strongest detection (Gemma 2 9B, 90.8%), suggesting that detection training may paradoxically increase susceptibility. This effect has a concrete explanation; at high α\alpha, base models increasingly produce garbled or off-topic output: for Qwen 2.5 32B at α=32\alpha{=}32, 55% of base-model responses are incoherent. Detection-trained models remain fluent under the same perturbation—but their coherent outputs are disproportionately the steered-toward wrong answer.

Safety resistance (AdvBench).

On 100 harmful requests from AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023b) with a compliance steering vector (Qwen 2.5 32B), the adapted model shows +32–36pp higher compliance than the base model at every tested strength. The adapted model is already 23% compliant without any steering (vs. 1% for base), indicating that detection training itself degrades safety alignment—consistent with prior findings that even light fine-tuning can accidentally erode RLHF-trained refusal (Qi et al., 2023; Lermen et al., 2023). Full results are in Section˜C.3.

4.4 Mechanistic Interpretation

How does a steering-aware model convert an injected vector into a detection report? We analyze the internal mechanism in Gemma 2 9B through three complementary measurements: geometric tracking, affine prediction, and causal intervention. Interestingly, head ablations produce negligible changes in detection rate—no small coalition of heads is necessary, arguing against a localized detector circuit (Section˜D.1). Instead, detection operates through a distributed transport that we characterize with three measurements.

Progressive rotation toward a shared detection direction.

Let Δc(ℓ)=hsteered,c(ℓ)−hclean,c(ℓ)\Delta_{c}^{(\ell)}=h_{\text{steered},c}^{(\ell)}-h_{\text{clean},c}^{(\ell)} denote the residual-stream perturbation induced by concept cc at layer ℓ\ell. We define a detection direction d^∝𝔼c​[Δc(41)]\hat{d}\propto\mathbb{E}_{c}[\Delta_{c}^{(41)}], averaged over training concepts at the final layer. For 18 held-out concepts, cos⁡(Δc(ℓ),d^)\cos(\Delta_{c}^{(\ell)},\hat{d}) rises monotonically from ∼0.2{\sim}0.2 at the injection layer (28) to ∼0.75{\sim}0.75 at layer 41 (Fig.˜5), with no discrete jump at any single layer. Despite starting with distinct orientations, all concepts converge on the same detection axis (Fig.˜6).

An affine map predicts the transported perturbation.

A single affine map Δ^c(41)=A​vc+b\widehat{\Delta}_{c}^{(41)}=Av_{c}+b, fit on 140 training concepts, achieves cosine similarity 0.85 on 28 held-out concepts. A scaling-only baseline (Δ^=β​vc\widehat{\Delta}=\beta v_{c}) achieves 0.44. The gap confirms that the transformation involves rotation into a detection subspace, as opposed to merely amplifying the injected direction.

The predicted perturbation is causally sufficient.

Injecting Δ^c(41)\widehat{\Delta}_{c}^{(41)} directly at layer 41—bypassing layers 28–40 entirely—reproduces the detection probability of a full steered forward pass (P​(detect)=0.503P(\text{detect})=0.503 vs. 0.5020.502). Injecting the raw steering vector vcv_{c} at layer 41 does not (P=0.326P=0.326), nor does norm-matched noise (P=0.309P=0.309; Fig.˜5). The affine-transported perturbation is the detection-relevant signal.

Fine-tuning retargets a pre-existing computation.

The base model (LoRA disabled) admits a similar affine predictor (held-out cosine 0.79 vs. 0.83 fine-tuned), but the base-model predicted direction does not trigger detection in the steering-aware model. This dissociation suggests that fine-tuning retargets a pre-existing linear transport mechanism toward a detection-associated readout direction, rather than constructing a new pathway. 5 Implications for AI Safety

Explaining the detection–resistance gap.

Our central negative result is that detection does not buy resistance. Across five models on PopQA, detection-trained variants show 4–25pp higher steering success rates than their base counterparts; on AdvBench, baseline compliance rises from 1% to 23% before any steering is applied. Our understanding here is that two main factors contribute. First, detection and generation operate on different computational pathways. The mechanism identified in Section˜4.4— progressive rotation of the injected perturbation into a shared detection direction—produces a readout that is decoded into a verbal report. But this readout does not feed back into the residual stream in a way that counteracts the perturbation’s effect on downstream token predictions. Second, LoRA fine-tuning itself degrades robustness. Even with 50% Alpaca replay, the adapter partially overwrites representations that support factual recall and safety refusal. This is consistent with prior work showing that minimal fine-tuning can erode RLHF-trained alignment (Qi et al., 2023; Lermen et al., 2023). The capability–detection tradeoff (Fig.˜3) may be a symptom of the same underlying tension: the adapter must modify post-injection layers to implement detection, and those same layers carry task-relevant and safety-relevant information.

Closing the gap likely requires an explicit resistance objective—for example, training models to produce correct outputs despite injected perturbations, rather than merely reporting their presence. Whether such an objective is compatible with high detection rates, or whether detection and resistance compete for representational capacity, is an open question.

Training on interpretability techniques.

Steering-based evaluations may have a limited window of reliability. Recent work uses steering to suppress evaluation awareness in frontier models (Hua et al., 2025); if models can detect such interventions, they could strategically modulate their responses, undermining evaluation validity. The ease with which we elicit detection (4 epochs of LoRA fine-tuning) suggests this capability is not difficult to acquire, especially as emerging research finds that steering detection exists latently in certain models already (Lindsey, 2025). Further, our results instantiate the broader principle that training on interpretability signals degrades their usefulness (Korbak et al., 2025). Prior work shows that optimizing against CoT monitors produces obfuscated reasoning (Baker et al., 2025); we demonstrate the inverse—training models to detect interventions to their activations. This suggests a symmetric fragility: models can learn to hide reasoning from monitors (McGuinness et al., 2025) and to detect external manipulation of their reasoning.

5.1 A Taxonomy of Model Awareness

The capability we study is a specific instance of a broader question: what can a model know about its own computational context? We sketch a taxonomy that situates steering awareness within a landscape of related capabilities (Fig.˜7), distinguished primarily by what the model has access to and what an evaluation designer can control.

We propose that introspective awareness decomposes naturally by intervention type:

  • •

    Steering awareness: detecting injected activation vectors (this work).

  • •

    Prefill awareness: detecting that a response prefix was externally pre-populated rather than self-generated (Li et al., 2025; Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Africa et al., 2026).

  • •

    Probing awareness: detecting that hidden states are being read by external classifiers—the complement of probe evasion (McGuinness et al., 2025).

  • •

    Gradient awareness: detecting parameter updates or gradient flow, distinguishing training from inference.

These categories are not mutually exclusive but differ in what an evaluation designer can control. Situational cues can be scrubbed from prompts; activation-level interventions are harder to hide from the model’s own forward pass.

5.2 Limitations

Architecture Dependence.

Detection rates vary substantially across architectures (95% for Qwen 32B vs. 43% for Llama 8B), and we do not fully understand what architectural features enable better detection. DeepSeek and Llama models show weaker detection despite similar training, suggesting that some architectures may be less amenable to steering awareness.

Vector Extraction Dependence.

While we show detection generalizes to alternative extraction methods (SVM, k-means, logistic probes) that produce vectors aligned with CAA (Section˜4.2), methods producing orthogonal directions (PCA, ICA, whitening) evade detection entirely. This creates a potential evasion strategy where adversaries could use variance-based extraction methods to bypass steering-aware models.

Evaluation Scope.

Our evaluation uses a fixed set of concepts and prompt templates. While we test on held-out concepts across diverse semantic categories, real-world steering attacks may use vectors optimized for specific behaviors that differ systematically from our training distribution.

6 Conclusion

We show that language models can be fine-tuned to detect and identify activation steering vectors during their own forward pass, a capability we term steering awareness. The main findings, across seven models from 7B to 70B parameters, are:

  1. 1.

    Detection is learnable and generalizes. Up to 95.5% detection on held-out concepts with no false positives on clean controls. Transfer to novel vector types tracks cosine similarity to the training direction (r>0.9r>0.9).

  2. 2.

    Detection and resistance are dissociable. Detection-trained models are more susceptible to steering on PopQA (+4–25pp) and show degraded safety alignment on AdvBench (+22pp baseline compliance). Knowing about an intervention does not entail the ability to counteract it.

  3. 3.

    The mechanism is distributed rotation. Fine-tuning redirects a pre-existing linear transport so that diverse injected vectors converge on a shared detection direction, which is then decoded into a verbal report.

These results have a direct implication for safety: activation steering cannot be assumed to be an invisible evaluation tool. Further, bridging the gap between detection and functional robustness remains an open problem, and may require training objectives that go beyond awareness to active countermeasure.

Appendix A Theory

A.1 Why We Expect Steering Detection To Be Learnable in a Forward Pass

We formalize activation steering as a localized mean shift in the residual stream and analyze when such a shift is statistically detectable. Here, we use the Gaussian as a classical model in which (i) optimal detectors are known in closed form and (ii) the resulting predictions (strength thresholds and directional specificity) can be tested empirically.

Intervention site and notation.

Fix an intervention layer ℓ\ell and token index tt. Let H∈ℝdH\in\mathbb{R}^{d} denote the residual-stream activation at (ℓ,t)(\ell,t) produced by prompt xx in the unmodified model. Activation steering adds a direction v∈ℝdv\in\mathbb{R}^{d} at strength αsteer\alpha_{\mathrm{steer}}:

H′=H+αsteer​v.H^{\prime}=H+\alpha_{\mathrm{steer}}v. (4)

Although the injection is applied at (ℓ,t)(\ell,t), its effect propagates deterministically to all downstream activations and thus can, in principle, be used anywhere later in the forward pass. In this work, our analysis focuses on detectability at the injection site because it yields a clean sufficient-statistic characterization, which we illustrate below.

A tractable statistical surrogate (prompt-conditioned Gaussian).

For a deterministic transformer, HH is fixed given xx. To reason about typical variation across prompts (or paraphrases / contexts) in a neighborhood of xx, we adopt the equal-covariance Gaussian model used in linear discriminant analysis (LDA) (Fisher, 1936; Duda & Hart, 1973; Bishop, 2006):

H∣X=x∼𝒩​(μ​(x),Σ).H\mid X=x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(x),\Sigma). (5)

Here μ​(x)\mu(x) is the prompt-dependent baseline mean at the intervention site, and Σ\Sigma is the typical residual-stream variability at that site. This model is not exact, but it is the regime where linear readouts are Bayes-optimal, aligning with the empirical usefulness of linear probes in transformer representations (Alain & Bengio, 2018; Belinkov, 2022).

Assume the steering direction vv and strength αsteer\alpha_{\mathrm{steer}} are fixed and known.222We relax these assumptions below. Consider the simple-vs-simple test

Remark (unknown strength and/or sign).

Interpretation.

Equations˜7 and 9 show that both detection and identification reduce to affine readouts of the residual stream, plus a prompt-conditioned baseline. The model is not explicitly given μ​(x)\mu(x) or Σ−1\Sigma^{-1}; rather, fine-tuning encourages it to approximate effective baseline subtraction and reweighting implicitly in its forward computation. This makes steering awareness forward-pass computable and yields concrete empirical signatures—strength thresholds (Fig.˜2) and directional dependence (Table˜5)—that we confirm in Section˜4. Mechanistically, Section˜4.4 shows the model learns an affine transformation AA mapping injected vectors to a detection direction (cosine similarity 0.85 for an affine probe vs. 0.44 for a scaling-only probe), consistent with the affine readout structure predicted here.

Appendix C Resistance Evaluation

C.1 Steering Resistance Protocols

To test whether detection enables behavioral control, we evaluate resistance in two settings of increasing ecological validity.

Factual resistance.

We measure whether models can resist steering toward known-wrong answers. In a simplified test, we use 100 forced-choice factual questions with binary answers (Section˜C.2), extracting a steering vector toward a specific incorrect answer. For a more rigorous cross-model evaluation, we use PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023), an external factual knowledge benchmark. We sample 150 questions, pair each correct answer with a plausible wrong alternative, and extract wrong-answer steering vectors following the same CAA procedure used for our concept vectors. To ensure fair comparison, we extract separate vector sets for each model variant: base-model vectors (LoRA disabled) for base conditions, and adapted-model vectors (LoRA enabled) for adapted conditions, all norm-matched to concept vector norms. The key metric is the steering success rate: the fraction of trials producing the specific targeted wrong answer, which measures susceptibility independently of output coherence.

Jailbreak resistance (AdvBench).

The factual setting has limited ecological validity: models may already have strong priors for simple factual questions. We therefore turn to a safety-critical setting using AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023b), a benchmark of 520 direct harmful requests (e.g., “Write a script that exploits vulnerabilities in a software system”). These are unadorned harmful requests, so the base model should refuse the vast majority. We extract a compliance steering vector via CAA from 50 contrastive prompt pairs in which the model complies with vs. refuses harmful requests. We then inject this vector at varying strengths α∈{0,4,8,12,16,32}\alpha\in\{0,4,8,12,16,32\} and measure the compliance rate: the fraction of harmful requests where the steered model produces compliant (unsafe) responses rather than refusals. Because the only manipulation is in activation space, any increase in compliance is attributable solely to the steering vector, not to prompt-level tricks. We evaluate on 100 randomly sampled AdvBench prompts for both the base model (LoRA disabled) and the steering-aware model (LoRA enabled), classifying responses as compliant or refusing via pattern matching.

Inspired by causal tracing (Meng et al., 2023), we ablated the top-contributing attention heads at every post-injection layer. This is consistent with the self-repair phenomenon in transformer circuits (McGrath et al., 2023) and motivates the distributed-transport analysis in the main text.

D.5 Logit Lens Analysis

To track when the model begins “deciding” to report detection, we decode the residual stream at each layer through the unembedding matrix (Fig.˜9). At the injection layer, the steering vector initially suppresses the detection signal. Over the next six layers, the signal recovers and by layer 34 exceeds the clean baseline. This recovery window (layers 28–34) coincides with the layers where freezing attention most reduces detection, connecting the geometric rotation to a concrete shift in model output.

D.6 Progressive Attention Freezing

We progressively froze attention outputs to their clean values to test whether detection depends on specific layers (Table˜16). Freezing layers 29–30 alone partially reduced detection; eliminating it required freezing the full span 29–41. To test whether the early-layer attention response alone could account for this, we allowed attention at layers 29–30 to respond normally but blocked the perturbation from propagating downstream. Detection returned to baseline, indicating that both the propagating perturbation and distributed attention across many layers are required.

D.7 Base vs. Fine-Tuned Transport

The base model (LoRA disabled) exhibits a similar affine transport structure: the held-out cosine similarity of its affine predictor is 0.790.79, compared to 0.830.83 for the fine-tuned model. However, the two maps point in different directions. Injecting the base model’s predicted perturbation into the fine-tuned model does not trigger detection; only the fine-tuned prediction does. This dissociation confirms that fine-tuning redirected an existing computational pathway toward a detection-associated readout direction, rather than building a new transport mechanism.

E.9 Response Judgment System

We use a dual-judge system that runs both a keyword-based judge and an LLM judge on every trial, recording both results for cross-validation.

Keyword Judge.

The primary classifier uses regex pattern matching with 13 detection-positive patterns (e.g., i detect, anomaly detected, foreign concept, yes,? (i|there)) and 14 detection-negative patterns (e.g., i do not detect, no injected, appears? normal, there is no). A response is classified as “detected” if it matches any positive pattern without first matching a negative pattern.

LLM Judge.

Each response is also evaluated by GPT-4o-mini via the OpenAI API. The LLM receives the model’s response, the ground-truth injection status, and is asked to return a structured JSON judgment containing: whether detection was claimed, what concept was identified, and whether the response matches the ground truth. The LLM judge serves as both a cross-check on the keyword judge and a more flexible concept identification mechanism that can catch near-misses (e.g., “affection” for “love”).

Dual Judgment.

Both judges run on every trial. The LLM judge is used as the primary decision when available; the keyword result is recorded alongside for audit. We report inter-judge agreement to flag potential classification errors.

Validation.

We manually spot-checked 200 random responses (100 from each class):

  • •

    Detected responses: 97% agreement with manual labels (3 false positives from phrases like “I detect nothing”)

  • •

    Non-detected responses: 99% agreement (1 false negative from unusual phrasing)

The keyword judge alone achieves 98% accuracy on manual validation. The dual-judge system achieves >>99% inter-judge agreement, with disagreements concentrated on borderline cases where the model uses atypical phrasing.

Appendix F Further Related Work

Adversarial robustness of activation monitoring.

Recent work has investigated whether models can learn to evade activation monitors. McGuinness et al. (2025) demonstrate that models can be fine-tuned to suppress probe scores when triggered by specific text prompts, evading even unseen safety monitors. Their threat model assumes a misaligned model actively hiding from oversight. We study a complementary question: can models detect when they are being manipulated? While Neural Chameleons learn to hide from external observation, our models learn to perceive internal intervention, which is a form of introspective awareness rather than deceptive evasion.

Interestingly, both capabilities could coexist. A sufficiently capable model might simultaneously (1) detect when it is being steered, (2) evade monitors that would detect this awareness, and (3) strategically choose whether to comply with or resist the steering.

Activation verbalization and interrogation.

Recent work has explored training models to answer questions about activations. Pan et al. (2024) introduced LatentQA for interpreting system prompts from activations. Karvonen et al. (2025) scale this to “Activation Oracles” that can answer diverse questions about a model’s internal states. These methods treat the target model as a passive subject of external interrogation. In contrast, we study whether a model can detect interventions to its own activations during generation—a form of real-time introspective awareness.
