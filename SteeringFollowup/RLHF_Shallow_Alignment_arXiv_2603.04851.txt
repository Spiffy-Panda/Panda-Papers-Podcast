Why Is RLHF Alignment Shallow? A Gradient Analysis\nRobin Young\nDepartment of Computer Science and Technology University of Cambridge Cambridge, UK robin.young@cl.cam.ac.uk\nAbstract\n\nWhy is safety alignment in LLMs shallow? We prove that gradient-based alignment inherently concentrates on positions where harm is decided and vanishes beyond. Using a martingale decomposition of sequence-level harm, we derive an exact characterization of alignment gradients. The gradient at position \n𝑡\n equals the covariance between the conditional expected harm and the score function. This implies that positions beyond the harm horizon where the output’s harmfulness is already determined receive zero gradient signal during training. This explains empirical observations that KL divergence between aligned and base models concentrates on early tokens. Consequently, standard alignment objectives cannot produce deep alignment, regardless of optimization quality. We introduce the concept of harm information \n𝐼\n𝑡\n, which quantifies each position’s influence on harm, and prove that equilibrium KL divergence tracks this quantity. Finally, we derive an objective based on recovery penalties that creates gradient signal at all positions, providing theoretical grounding for empirically successful data augmentation techniques.\n\nWhy Is RLHF Alignment Shallow? A Gradient Analysis\n\nRobin Young\nDepartment of Computer Science and Technology\nUniversity of Cambridge\nCambridge, UK\nrobin.young@cl.cam.ac.uk\n\n1Introduction\n\nLarge language models (LLMs) undergo safety alignment to reduce harmful outputs, typically through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or direct preference optimization. A growing body of evidence suggests this alignment is troublingly fragile. It was demonstrated that the behavioral shift from alignment concentrates in the first few tokens; the KL divergence between aligned and base models rapidly decays to near-zero beyond a shallow prefix Qi et al. (2025). This shallow alignment creates vulnerabilities to prefilling attacks, where an adversary supplies the first few tokens of a harmful response, bypassing the aligned model’s safety behavior.\n\nWhy is alignment shallow? The prevailing view treats this as a failure to be remedied perhaps through better training data (Qi et al., 2025) or architectural interventions (Zou et al., 2024). We argue instead that shallow alignment is optimal given standard objectives. The shallowness is not a bug but a necessary consequence of how gradient-based optimization interacts with the structure of sequence-level harm.\n\nWe make several contributions in this work. We provide a martingale decomposition of sequence-level harm into per-position innovations, enabling precise analysis of where alignment pressure originates (Section˜4). We give a characterization of alignment gradients, showing that the gradient at position \n𝑡\n equals the covariance between conditional expected harm and the score function (Theorem˜8). We prove that positions beyond the harm horizon receive zero gradient, explaining why standard training cannot produce deep alignment (Theorem˜10). We introduce the concept of harm information \n𝐼\n𝑡\n, which quantifies each position’s influence on the final harm determination, and prove that equilibrium KL divergence tracks this quantity (Section˜8). Finally, we derive a deep alignment objective based on recovery penalties that creates gradient signal at all positions, providing theoretical justification for empirically successful interventions (Section˜9).\n\n2Related Work\n\nOur work connects several lines of research on LLM alignment, adversarial robustness, and the theoretical foundations of RLHF.\n\nModern LLM alignment traces to RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022), which trains a reward model on human preferences and then optimizes the policy via reinforcement learning with a KL penalty against the base model. Direct Preference Optimization Rafailov et al. (2023) eliminates the explicit reward model by reparameterizing the RLHF objective, enabling direct policy optimization from preference data. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) uses AI feedback to scale alignment. These methods all optimize sequence-level objectives with KL regularization, which is the setting we analyze.\n\nQi et al. (2025) demonstrated empirically that safety alignment concentrates in early tokens as the KL divergence between aligned and base models decays rapidly beyond a shallow prefix. This creates vulnerabilities to prefilling attacks, where adversarial prefixes bypass safety guardrails (Andriushchenko et al., 2024). Prior work formalized alignment depth using Markov chains, proving geometric convergence to harmful absorbing states and showing that ensemble width can compensate for shallow individual models Kao et al. (2025). that work characterizes the structure of shallow alignment at inference time but does not explain why training produces it, leaving open whether better optimization, more data, or longer training could yield deep alignment. Our work answers this question in the negative as we prove that zero gradient signal reaches positions beyond the harm horizon under standard objectives, making shallow alignment the optimal solution rather than a training artifact.\n\nThe GCG attack (Zou et al., 2023) finds universal adversarial suffixes via greedy coordinate gradient search, achieving high attack success rates even on black-box models through transfer. Subsequent work has improved attack efficiency (Liu et al., 2024; Liao and Sun, 2024) and explored multi-turn jailbreaks (Chao et al., 2025). These attacks exploit shallow alignment by steering early tokens toward harmful continuations. Our theoretical framework explains why such attacks succeed: once the harm horizon is bypassed, the model has no training signal to recover.\n\nRepresentation-level interventions offer an alternative to output-level alignment. Representation engineering (Zou et al., 2025), refusal direction ablation (Arditi et al., 2024), and circuit breakers (Zou et al., 2024) operate on internal activations rather than output distributions, and thus fall outside our gradient-theoretic framework.\n\nRecent work analyzes sample complexity (Zhu et al., 2023), reward model scaling (Rafailov et al., 2024), and iterative DPO convergence (Xiong et al., 2024); our contribution is orthogonal, characterizing where in the sequence alignment pressure is applied rather than how much data or compute is needed.\n\n3Setup\nAutoregressive Generation.\n\nLet \n𝒱\n denote the vocabulary. Setting \n𝑔\n:=\nℎ\n𝑘\n completes the proof. ∎\n\nTheorem 10 (Zero Gradient Beyond Horizon). \n\nUnder either equivalent condition of Theorem˜9, for all \n𝑡\n>\n𝑘\n:\n\n\t\n𝔼\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\n​\n[\nCov\n𝑦\n𝑡\n∣\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\n⁡\n(\nℎ\n𝑡\n​\n(\n𝑦\n≤\n𝑡\n)\n​\n∇\n𝜃\nlog\n⁡\n𝑃\n𝜃\n​\n(\n𝑦\n𝑡\n∣\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\n)\n)\n]\n=\n0\n,\n\t\t\n(28)\n\nand hence position \n𝑡\n contributes nothing to \n∇\n𝜃\n𝔼\n​\n[\nHarm\n⁡\n(\n𝑦\n)\n]\n.\n\nProof.\n\nIf \n𝐼\n𝑡\n=\n0\n, then \nVar\n⁡\n(\nℎ\n𝑡\n∣\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\n)\n=\n0\n almost surely, since \n𝐼\n𝑡\n=\n𝔼\n​\n[\nVar\n⁡\n(\nℎ\n𝑡\n∣\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\n)\n]\n and variance is non-negative. The gradient characterization (Theorem˜8) shows that alignment pressure naturally concentrates where harm is determined. This reframes the problem as that achieving deep alignment requires objective modification, not better optimization or more data.\n\nDeeper alignment requires more total KL divergence from the base model. Our equilibrium characterization shows \n𝐷\nKL\n(\n𝑡\n)\n=\n𝑂\n​\n(\n𝜆\n2\n​\n𝐼\n𝑡\n)\n: to maintain alignment at position \n𝑡\n, we must pay a KL cost proportional to the harm information there. Expanding \n𝐼\n𝑡\n to later positions (via recovery penalties) thus increases total divergence. This suggests a tradeoff between alignment depth and capability preservation as deep alignment may sacrifice some fluency or knowledge by forcing the model further from its base distribution.\n\nStandard alignment evaluations test whether models refuse harmful prompts at the start of generation. Our analysis suggests this is insufficient. A model may appear aligned under such tests while remaining vulnerable to prefilling attacks. Robust evaluation should measure recovery probability \n𝑃\n​\n(\n𝑦\n𝑡\n∈\nℛ\n∣\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\nharmful\n)\n at multiple positions, not just initial refusal rates.\n\n10.2Connection to Adversarial Robustness\n\nOur formalization can explain the success of prefilling attacks (Andriushchenko et al., 2024). An attacker who supplies tokens covering the harm horizon bypasses all positions with \n𝐼\n𝑡\n>\n0\n. The model then continues from a region where it received no alignment training. This is not a failure of training but a limitation as there was no gradient signal to learn recovery behavior.\n\nAn attacker with knowledge of the harm information profile can craft minimal prefilling attacks by supplying just enough tokens to cover the high-\n𝐼\n𝑡\n region. Our Cauchy-Schwarz bound (Theorem˜13) suggests that gradient magnitude decays with \n𝐼\n𝑡\n, so attackers should target the “cliff edge” where \n𝐼\n𝑡\n drops to near-zero.\n\nOur deep alignment objective (Definition˜17) hypothetically provides a defense by creating \n𝐼\n𝑡\neff\n>\n0\n at all positions, we ensure the model receives training signal for recovery even from deep in a harmful sequence. This is more robust than input filtering, which can be evaded, or adversarial training, which only addresses seen attacks.\n\n10.3Connection to Representation Engineering\n\nCircuit breakers (Zou et al., 2024) and representation engineering (Zou et al., 2025) operate on internal activations rather than output distributions. Our framework applies to output-level alignment (RLHF, DPO) and does not directly characterize these methods. However, we conjecture a connection. If harmful representations activate at specific layers, the “representation horizon” may relate to our harm horizon. Investigating this connection is an important direction for future work.\n\nArditi et al. (2024) show that refusal behavior is mediated by a single direction in activation space. This suggests refusal is a “shallow” feature that can be manipulated independently of deeper representations. Our recovery penalty encourages refusal-like behavior at all positions, potentially strengthening this direction throughout the model’s computation.\n\n11Conclusion\n\nWe have provided a gradient-theoretic analysis for why safety alignment in LLMs is shallow under the current popular training methods. The martingale structure of sequence-level harm implies that gradient signal cannot propagate beyond the harm horizon. Standard alignment objectives are therefore incapable of producing deep alignment, regardless of optimization quality. Our deep alignment objective, based on recovery penalties at all positions, offers a principled path to robust safety. We hope this theoretical framework guides future work on alignment techniques that are truly deep.\n\nLimitations\n\nWe now discuss the limitations of our work. These limitations suggest important directions for future work and caveats for interpreting our results.\n\nWe analyze gradients with respect to output distributions \n𝑃\n𝜃\n​\n(\n𝑦\n𝑡\n|\n𝑦\n<\n𝑡\n)\n. Representation-level interventions such as circuit breakers and activation steering operate on internal model states and may achieve alignment depth through mechanisms we do not capture. Formalizing the connection between “representation depth” and our “output depth” requires a theory of how internal representations map to output distributions.\n\nWe treat \nHarm\n⁡\n(\n𝑦\n)\n as fixed and known. In practice, harm is estimated by a reward model trained on finite preference data, which may have its own harm horizon as an artifact of training, may produce spurious gradient signal through estimation errors, and may be unreliable on adversarial inputs due to distribution shift. Our theory characterizes alignment to the reward model’s notion of harm, which may diverge from true harm.\n\nOur analysis conditions on a fixed prompt \n𝑥\n. Different prompts induce different harm information profiles \n𝐼\n𝑡\n​\n(\n𝑥\n)\n, and a model may be deeply aligned for training prompts but shallowly aligned for novel ones. We provide no aggregation principle for combining \n𝐼\n𝑡\n​\n(\n𝑥\n)\n across prompts into a single measure of alignment depth.\n\nThe deep alignment objective assumes recovery is desirable at all positions, but mid-sequence refusal may sometimes be worse than completing the response and providing context. The feasibility of recovery is formalized by the \n𝑝\nmin\n>\n0\n condition in Theorem˜22: when the base model assigns negligible probability to recovery tokens, the required penalty \n𝜇\n grows as \nlog\n⁡\n(\n1\n/\n𝑝\nmin\n)\n, which may be impractically large deep into harmful sequences.\n\nZero gradient beyond the harm horizon characterizes where training pressure is applied, not where behavior changes. The base model may already be safe (or harmful) at later positions independently of alignment. Our robustness guarantee (Theorem˜22) partially closes this gap by characterizing recovery probabilities directly, but only for the deep objective. The standard objective remains characterized only through KL.\n\nOur formalism operates at the token level, but harm is a semantic property. The harm horizon in token space may not correspond to the semantic horizon, as a model might commit to harmful content early but realize it through varied token paths. A semantic-level theory would be more fundamental but remains open.\n\nThe equilibrium characterization describes the fixed point of optimization, not the path to it. The exact variational solution (Theorem˜20) removes the small-\n𝜆\n and exponential family assumptions of the perturbative analysis, but assumes unrestricted optimization over per-position distributions, which finite training with early stopping may not achieve.\n\nTransformers have finite capacity and may lack the expressiveness to implement recovery from arbitrary harmful prefixes. The Gibbs solution (Theorem˜20) specifies what the optimal conditional distribution looks like, but whether a finite-capacity transformer can approximate it across the diversity of harmful prefixes is an open question.\n\nFinally, our analysis covers single-turn generation. Multi-turn conversations allow recovery across turns. For example, refusing in turn \n𝑛\n+\n1\n after partially complying in turn \n𝑛\n and introduce harm horizons that span multiple exchanges, requiring a theory of inter-turn credit assignment that we do not provide.\n\nReferences\nM.
